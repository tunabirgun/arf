Structure

Links & the graph

A note becomes useful the moment it stops being alone.

Organization in Arf is a hybrid, because that is what researchers actually use in practice. You get folders and subfolders for coarse structure, concept hashtags for categories, and links for the real conceptual relationships between ideas. None of them is mandatory; you can save a note with no folder and no tag and only connect it later.

Links that survive a rename

You link one note to another by writing its title in double brackets, [[Pointer states]], or [[Pointer states|shown text]] to show different words. Arf keeps track of the relationship in both directions. The note you point to lists the link under Referenced in in the side panel, so you discover the connections you forgot you made.

The important detail is under the surface. Every note carries a stable identifier that never changes, and links resolve to that identifier rather than to a filename. Rename a note in Arf and every [[link]] to it is rewritten to the new title; rename or move the file on disk and the link still finds it by id.

Portable by default

[[Wikilinks]] are the same syntax Obsidian and many other Markdown tools use, so a vault opened elsewhere keeps its links readable, and an Obsidian vault imported into Arf keeps its links working.

Previews and context

Rest the pointer on a link in the reading view and a small card shows the linked note's title, the first lines of its text, and how many links it has in and out; click the card to open the note, or to create it if it does not exist yet. The same card appears when a link receives keyboard focus, and for the notes Resonance suggests in the side panel.

Each backlink under Referenced in shows the sentence in which the other note mentions this one, with the link in bold. You can see why a note points here without opening it.

Tags for concepts

A hashtag like #decoherence groups notes by concept, and tags nest — #method/statistics — so you can be as coarse or as fine as you like. Clicking a tag filters your notes to it. Tags and folders answer different questions: a folder is where a note lives, a tag is what it is about, and a link is how it relates to another idea.

The knowledge graph

The everyday graph is local: in the side panel it shows the note you are reading, the notes one link away in either direction, and, as dashed lines, the unlinked notes Resonance suggests. The full-window graph (Ctrl/⌘+G, or Full above the local graph) shows the whole vault.

The layout is a force simulation: linked notes attract, all notes repel, and the arrangement settles and then stops moving.

Move

Drag and pin

Drag a note and the notes linked to it are pulled along. Release with Shift held, or double-click, to pin a note where it is; pins are remembered, and Unpin all releases them.

Look

Zoom and pan

Zoom toward the cursor with the wheel or a pinch, drag the background to pan (it glides to a stop), and hover a note to light up its neighbours while the rest recedes.

Find

Search

Type in Find a note and press Enter to zoom to the first match. Ctrl-click (⌘-click on a Mac) selects several notes at once.

The toolbar narrows the view: a depth control shows only the notes 1, 2 or 3 links from the open note (or the whole vault); tag and folder filters keep one part of the vault; Hide unlinked drops orphans; and Suggested links turns the dashed edges on or off. The Forces panel adjusts repulsion, link distance, link strength and centring, and the view can be saved as a PNG or SVG image.

The graph works from the keyboard too. Arrow keys move from a note to the linked note in that direction, Enter opens it, P pins or unpins it, +, − and 0 zoom and fit, and Escape clears the selection. With reduced motion turned on in your system, the layout is computed before it is shown and nothing animates.

The graph reads in ink, not colour. A node's size grows with its number of links, a filled circle has connections while a hollow one is still an orphan, and a suggestion is a dashed rather than a solid line. Nothing depends on colour alone.
